Why it is trusted

05Alerting that earns attention — with an owner and a clock13:00 · 3 min
“An alert that fires on ordinary traffic is worse than no alert at all. It teaches the team to ignore the screen.”
  • 182 rules across both businesses. Not one of them fires without a written next step. Say that twice — the discipline, not the count.
  • We measured, for every rule, how often its own condition was already true. A rule true 30% of the time describes a normal Tuesday, not an incident. Open Fixed incidents went from 14 to 1 without making a single rule weaker.
  • One signal, one incident, at the worst severity seen. Before, one outage could page three people from three queues.
  • New this month — open #alerts, one incident, the drawer: every incident has an owning team from one registry (17 teams: business × domain × level), and next to our acknowledgement SLA sit the contract clocks of the vendor — TCS: respond in 15 minutes, restore in 4 hours, RCA in 48 hours for a P1; Sigma: 10 minutes, 2 hours. Reminders escalate P1 to P4; management is copied at the third reminder.
  • Read the live MTTA and MTTR from the Alerts page rather than the slide — they are 30-day averages, per business (say “average”, not “median”; the two cards on the slide now say so), then: “measured from detection, not from when someone noticed.”

07AI inside operations — Yusr, two agents, Mission control18:00 · 3.5 min

If they ask · the short answers (the full list of CIO questions is in the project doc)

“What did it cost, and what does it cost to run?”
Built in-house by the existing team alongside delivery work — no licence, no vendor contract, no professional services. Running it is two VMs on our own estate and the people's time; the AI runs on a CPU server today, and the one hardware ask is a GPU host. The alternative was a product that does not know a Salam journey, plus building the mapping anyway.
“What happens if you leave? Is this one person's tool?”
Fair question, and the honest answer is that it is a risk we are reducing on purpose. Everything is documented in the repository, the runbooks sit on the rules themselves, ownership sits in the registry, and the next phase is about making the platform operable by the team rather than by its author — two engineers, the enablement sessions already run for L1 and L2. That is part of what I am asking you to back.
“Why not buy an observability product — Dynatrace, AppDynamics, Splunk?”
They watch infrastructure — CPU, latency, errors — and they are good at it; we may still want one under the platform. What they do not ship is a Salam journey: a port-in, a Nafath check, an SDA dealer order, a QR referral, a refund owed. That mapping is the value, and we would have built it on top of any product. Zero licence is the bonus, not the reason.
“How do I know the vendor figures are fair — TCS and Sigma will dispute them.”
Every number links to the incidents behind it, with timestamps from our own timers; the contract clauses are quoted next to the actual; blips under five minutes are excluded; the penalty is labelled a candidate until the monthly review. The point is not to punish — it is that the vendor conversation now starts from a shared list rather than two decks. TCS has been invited to calibrate the P1 rules with us.
“Is customer data safe in an AI system?”
Inference runs on our own infrastructure — nothing leaves Salam. Data is masked before it reaches a model; a real value is revealed only by a named person, and that reveal is audited. The models propose; they never decide, never act, never write to a system of record. Token ceilings per person and per agent, every call logged with its cost.
“The refund desk — who is accountable if the agent is wrong?”
The same people as today, with better evidence. The agent reviews and proposes; the approver approves by name on the page; L2 posts the refund in proxycms; the console closes the case only when the register shows it. The approvers, the executing team and the two clocks are configuration in Teams management, and the audit trail carries every step. A wrong proposal costs a click, not money.
“How do I know these numbers are real?”
Every figure on these slides comes out of the running platform, and I can open it live in this room. That is rather the point.
“Can this be sold outside Salam — and who would own it?”
Salam would. The proposal is that Salam packages and sells it, with the operations team that built it leading the product line. The Kingdom's operators share every national integration we already watch, and none of the market products know a KSA journey. Today's ask is the mandate to scope it properly: legal, brand and the commercial model. The synthetic demo tenant exists, so nothing of Salam's leaves the building in a pitch.
“Is the 1.4 M on slide 13 real money?”
Not yet — it is the contractual ceiling: credits are capped at 10 % of the fees, and the two L1/L2 contracts run at ≈1.16 M SAR a month, so the most the clauses allow is ≈1.4 M a year. What is real today is the measurement: every obligation timed by our clocks, per incident, with the clause next to the actual. A figure becomes money the month Finance validates the eligible fee and the vendor agrees the breach — that is why the slide says candidate. The same 10 % appears twice on purpose, once as credits when they miss and once as scope when we renew; they are two different conversations and the deck never adds them together.
“What does the Call Centre pay, then?”
An internal charge-back per seat, set with Salam B2B by the taskforce — no figure today. What is certain is what it does not pay: a per-seat licence and an integration project, because the journeys, Customer 360, view-as-user and the roles already exist. The price list is the same one Salam B2B would sell outside, which is the point of starting inside.
“Why do we need a GPU at all — can't the CPU do it?”
The agents already wait on the CPU: an incident triage note takes about 12 seconds, one at a time; the log-signature backlog takes hours to drain; Yusr queues behind the agents during an incident storm. The GPU runs the 32B model class we want for incident analysis, nine streams at once, and nothing leaves our network. Before any purchase there is a free step this week — a small model for the mechanical duties on the current server — but it buys speed, not concurrency. The GPU is the destination; the CPU step is the bridge.
“What exactly is blocking the GPU node?”
At the 18 Sep validation: no NVIDIA device passed through to the VM (VMware DirectPath I/O or a vGPU profile), 8 vCPU and 15 GB instead of 16 / 32, the new 250 GB disk not mounted as /data, Python 3.6 instead of 3.11, no driver, CUDA or container runtime, and no route to the model and package repositories. The network is ready. It needs one ticket with an owner and an ETA — and if the estate cannot pass a GPU through, a clear “no” this week so we switch to the CPU-only design or a rental bridge instead of waiting.
“What would commercialising cost Salam — and who supports a customer?”
This quarter: a taskforce's time, a legal review and a brand decision. Hosting for a first customer is one dedicated VM in a Saudi region, or their own servers. Support is the same L1/L2 model we run for ourselves, sold as a subscription by subscriber band — the taskforce prices it. No capex decision is on the table today, only whether we scope it.
“What is still missing?”
Answer straight — it buys credibility. Assisted resolution is not built. ServiceNow is not integrated. The Fixed refund desk has its definition but not its detectors. One Fixed data feed needs an owner. Coverage of Fixed is newer than Mobile and will need tuning for another month. None of that is a reason to wait to make it official.
